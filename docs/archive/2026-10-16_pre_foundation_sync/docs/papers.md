## Core Web-Agent Papers

#### AutoWebGLM: A Large Language Model-based Web Navigating Agent

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../AutoWebGLM_A_Large_Language_Model-based_Web_Navigating_Agent.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/autowebglm_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, KDD 2024 / arXiv.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** AutoWebGLM is a web-navigation capability system and a core/root inventory paper; it also introduces the bilingual AutoWebBench evaluation benchmark.
- **Problem and motivation:** Real-world browser agents must reason over verbose, noisy HTML, support diverse operations, and complete multi-step tasks across websites with unfamiliar interaction logic. The paper seeks practical, open web navigation with a compact 6B model rather than dependence on very large proprietary models.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** Built on ChatGLM3-6B, AutoWebGLM uses pruned HTML, OCR support, viewport position, action history, and a unified ten-operation action space. Its approximately 10,000 real browsing traces support curriculum SFT, self-sampled DPO from roughly 13,000 preference examples, and environment-specific rejection-sampling finetuning; AutoWebBench provides English and Chinese cross-task and cross-domain splits with 50 human-verified traces per split.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** Evaluation covers AutoWebBench, Mind2Web, MiniWoB++—56 tasks with 100 episodes each—and WebArena, with comparisons against GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude 2, open LLMs, and specialized web agents. MiniWoB++ and WebArena receive separate environment-specific finetuning based on approximately 15,000 successful MiniWoB++ traces and 240 WebArena traces.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** AutoWebGLM achieved 64.8 and 58.6 step success rate on AutoWebBench’s English cross-task and cross-domain splits, and 65.4 and 61.8 on the corresponding Chinese splits, versus GPT-4’s best split score of 39.7. It averaged 59.5 on Mind2Web and reached success rates of 89.3 on MiniWoB++ and 18.2 on WebArena; environment-specific rejection-sampling finetuning raised the latter two results from 80.8 and 8.5, respectively.
- **Contribution:** The paper demonstrates that a single open 6B model can deliver strong bilingual and cross-site browser navigation through executable hybrid human–AI traces, structured webpage observations, learning from self-generated mistakes, and environment-specific practice. It additionally contributes AutoWebBench, a unified browser interaction framework, an HTML-pruning method, and released code, model, and data.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Hallucinations account for 44% of analyzed errors, poor graphical recognition for 28%, context misinterpretation for 20%, and pop-ups for 8%; the HTML-centered design remains weak on images, icons, maps, animation, video, and unusual interaction logic. RFT depends on sandbox reward signals and separate environment-specific training, AutoWebBench contains only 50 traces per split, substantial human annotation was required, and the evaluation reports no confidence intervals, variability estimates, or significance tests.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** This is a capability system whose agent performs external browser actions, creating relevance to action integrity, hallucination, environmental manipulation, and unintended interactions, but the paper neither presents an attack nor evaluates a security defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for compact open-model browser navigation based on HTML pruning, explicit browser state and history, hybrid human–AI trajectories, self-sampled preference training, and environment-specific rejection-sampling finetuning; cite AutoWebBench when discussing bilingual evaluation across familiar and unseen websites.

#### SeeAct / GPT-4V(ision) is a Generalist Web Agent, if Grounded

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../GPT-4V(ision)_is_a_Generalist_Web_Agent_if_Grounded.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/seeact_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, ICML 2024.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** SEEACT is a web-agent capability system and grounding study; it belongs in the core/root inventory as evidence about multimodal planning, executable action grounding, and generalization across unfamiliar websites.
- **Problem and motivation:** Rendered webpages compactly preserve visual and spatial meaning that raw HTML may obscure, but a web agent must convert a sound natural-language plan into an exact target element, operation, and value. The paper tests whether GPT-4V can serve as a generalist web agent and isolates fine-grained action grounding as the principal obstacle.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** SEEACT uses the task, action history, and current screenshot to generate one next action, then grounds that intention through element-attribute matching, ranked textual choices, image annotations, or human oracle grounding. The work also creates Multimodal Mind2Web by aligning verified screenshots with Mind2Web HTML and traces, and implements a Playwright-based framework for evaluating agents on live websites.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** Mind2Web contains more than 2,000 tasks across 137 websites, 31 low-level domains, and 12 high-level domains, with Cross-Task, Cross-Website, and Cross-Domain splits; several large-model and grounding experiments use 30 tasks per split. Online evaluation covers 90 live tasks—30 from each split—with human completion judgments and monitoring that blocks login, purchases, final submissions, profile changes, and other consequential actions.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** With textual-choice grounding, GPT-4V achieves 39.1%, 32.7%, and 42.0% offline step success on the Cross-Task, Cross-Website, and Cross-Domain subsets, versus 61.9%, 65.0%, and 62.1% with human grounding—a 22.8–32.3 percentage-point grounding gap. On the 90-task live evaluation, SEEACTChoice completes 37.8% of tasks and oracle-grounded SEEACT completes 51.1%, compared with strict offline whole-task success of 3.3% and 13.3%, respectively, showing that trace matching misses alternative routes and recovery.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a screenshot-driven generalist web agent that explicitly separates planning from grounding, systematically demonstrates that grounding—not merely high-level reasoning—is GPT-4V’s central bottleneck, and establishes textual HTML choices as the strongest of three tested automatic grounding strategies. It additionally contributes Multimodal Mind2Web and a live-browser evaluation framework that expose the discrepancy between reference-trace accuracy and actual task completion.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Oracle grounding is human-dependent and only an upper-bound diagnostic; automatic errors compound on long tasks, while textual choices collapse identical HTML elements, image annotations induce label hallucinations and spatial-linking errors, and attribute matching is heuristic. Some results use 30-task-per-split subsets, online evaluation covers only 90 monitored tasks and depends on mutable websites and human judgments, training screenshots may contain capture problems, and the exclusion of consequential actions prevents the results from establishing safe autonomous deployment.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role, not an evaluated defense.** Screenshot-based planning and DOM grounding expose agents to deceptive interfaces, visual or label manipulation, and cascading misclicks; the need for human approval of consequential actions also provides direct evidence that capable generalist agents require safeguards against harmful or privacy-sensitive operations.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when motivating the separation between web-action planning and executable grounding, documenting GPT-4V’s generalist navigation capability under reliable grounding, comparing multimodal grounding strategies, or explaining why strict offline trace evaluation can underestimate live web-agent success.

#### LASER: LLM Agent with State-Space Exploration for Web Navigation

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../LASER_LLM_Agent_with_State-Space_Exploration_for_Web_Navigation.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/laser_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2023, arXiv / OpenReview.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** LASER is a capability system for structured web navigation and a core/root inventory paper. It focuses on shopping tasks and uses explicit state-space exploration rather than a forward-only action policy.
- **Problem and motivation:** Demonstration-driven web agents may not learn how to recover from unfamiliar mistakes, while a global action space permits invalid context-dependent actions. LASER addresses these problems by making backtracking explicit and restricting decisions to actions valid in the current webpage state.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** LASER represents WebShop navigation with four manually defined states—Search, Results, Item, and Finish—each paired with state-specific instructions and permissible actions. GPT-4-0613 generates a rationale and selects a function-call action, while a memory buffer and backup strategy retain previously inspected items and force a historical selection near the 15-transition limit.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The main WebShop experiment used 500 test instructions over a simulated catalog of 1,181,436 Amazon-derived items, with success rate and 0–100 reward as metrics. Transfer was evaluated on the first 100 instructions against live Amazon content converted into WebShop’s textual representation, while ablations used 200 instructions and failure analysis covered 30 development-set errors.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** On WebShop, LASER achieved 50.0% success and 75.6 reward, compared with 45.0% and 67.5 for the strongest listed automated baseline by success rate, WebGUM; removing the backup strategy reduced LASER to 48.4% success and 71.2 reward. On the guarded Amazon transfer, LASER reached 62.0% success and 85.4 reward, versus 65.0% and 88.2 for manually evaluated humans.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces explicit, manually specified state-space exploration for zero-shot LLM web navigation, coupling state-conditioned instructions and action restrictions with recoverable transitions and memory-based fallback. It shows that this formulation can outperform demonstration-based automated systems on WebShop and transfer unchanged at the agent level to a textualized real Amazon site.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Evaluation is confined to product finding and selection; the states and prompts require manual annotation, and the Amazon study used textualized pages, manual scoring, only 100 instructions, and no actual purchases. Ablations covered 200 instructions, failure analysis covered 30 errors, no significance tests or uncertainty estimates were reported, performance degraded on long trajectories and with `text-davinci-003`, and retrieval failures accounted for 12 of the 30 analyzed errors.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role, not an evaluated defense.** State-specific action constraints and backtracking are relevant to controlling agent behavior, but LASER was not tested against adversarial webpages or prompt injection, and its imperfect success rates plus potentially irreversible real-world actions support retaining human approval for consequential operations such as purchasing.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite LASER when discussing planning- or search-based browser navigation, explicit state-conditioned action spaces, recovery from navigation errors, or zero-shot alternatives to ReAct-style trajectory demonstrations. It is also useful as evidence that structured simulation-to-real transfer can work under a normalized textual interface, with important qualifications about manual state design and guarded execution.

#### ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../ReAct_Synergizing_Reasoning_and_Acting_in_Language_Models.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/react_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2023, ICLR 2023.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** ReAct is a capability-system pattern and foundational agent architecture in the core/root inventory, providing adjacent background for later web agents.
- **Problem and motivation:** Reasoning-only prompting cannot verify claims or update knowledge through environmental feedback, so hallucinations can propagate, while action-only agents often lack explicit plans, task-state tracking, and mechanisms for recovering from failed actions. The paper asks whether interleaving reasoning and interaction can make both processes more effective, grounded, and interpretable.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** ReAct prompts a language model to alternate among free-form thoughts, task-specific actions, and environmental observations, using thoughts for planning, evidence synthesis, progress tracking, search reformulation, and exception handling. The authors evaluate few-shot PaLM-540B and GPT-3 agents, ReAct/chain-of-thought hybrids, ablations, and smaller PaLM models fine-tuned on 3,000 correctly answered trajectories.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** Evaluation covers HotpotQA multi-hop question answering, FEVER fact verification, 134 unseen ALFWorld household games, and 500 WebShop test instructions drawn from a simulated store containing 1.18 million products and 12,000 instructions. Knowledge tasks use a restricted Wikipedia search-and-lookup interface, while ALFWorld and WebShop expose predefined, non-live action spaces.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** On ALFWorld, the best ReAct prompt achieved 71% overall success, versus 45% for the controlled Act baseline and 37% for BUTLER. On WebShop, ReAct reached a 66.6 average attribute-satisfaction score and 40.0% full success, compared with Act’s 62.3 and 30.1%; on FEVER, ReAct scored 60.9% accuracy versus 56.3% for chain-of-thought, while the CoT-SC → ReAct hybrid reached 64.6%. In a manual HotpotQA analysis, hallucination accounted for 0% of 50 sampled ReAct failures versus 56% of 50 chain-of-thought failures, although ReAct had more reasoning errors.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces the ReAct thought–action–observation paradigm and demonstrates across knowledge retrieval, fact verification, household interaction, and simulated shopping that explicit reasoning can guide actions while observations ground subsequent reasoning. It also contributes controlled ablations, hybrid internal/external reasoning procedures, cross-model and fine-tuning evidence, a behavioral failure analysis, and an initial demonstration of correcting an agent by editing its thoughts.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** ReAct depends on human-authored demonstrations, is prompt-sensitive, is difficult for smaller models to learn through few-shot prompting, and can become rigid, loop, or fail after poor retrieval; unhelpful searches caused 23% of its sampled HotpotQA failures, and reasoning errors caused 47%. Results lack reported significance tests, use largely inaccessible PaLM models and restricted benchmark environments, can be distorted by ambiguous or outdated exact-match labels, remain below supervised knowledge systems and expert WebShop users, and provide only anecdotal evidence for human thought editing.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role, not an evaluated defense.** ReAct is a capability system whose reasoning–action loop underlies later web agents, but exposing external actions and incorporating environmental observations creates opportunities for malicious content, privacy violations, inappropriate information access, and harmful actions; the paper acknowledges such risks but evaluates only constrained Wikipedia, WebShop, and simulated-household environments without live purchases or unrestricted web access.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper as the foundational formulation and empirical evaluation of interleaved natural-language reasoning, environmental action, and observation in language-model agents, especially when describing the ReAct loop used by later web agents.

#### WebLINX: Real-World Website Navigation with Multi-Turn Dialogue

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../WebLINX_Real-World_Website_Navigation_with_Multi-Turn_Dialogue.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/weblinx_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, ICML 2024.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** Dataset and benchmark with evaluated navigation models; core/root inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Existing web-navigation benchmarks commonly use simplified environments, single-turn instructions, narrow domains, or autonomous execution of fully specified tasks. WEBLINX instead targets real-world browser use in which objectives evolve through dialogue and an agent must act, request missing information, and respond to revised instructions.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** WEBLINX contains 2,337 expert demonstrations and more than 100,000 actions and utterances across 155 real websites, with aligned dialogue, DOM trees, screenshots, element boxes, browser events, and page states. It adds turn-level action-specific metrics, Optimal Text Representation with structured truncation, and Dense Markup Ranking (DMR), which retrieves the ten most relevant HTML elements for action prediction.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The benchmark averages 43 turns and 1,775 HTML elements per page and spans 15 geographic areas, eight broad categories, and 50 subcategories. Models are evaluated on IID data and four OOD conditions covering unseen websites, unseen subcategories, held-out geographies, and instructors who cannot see the browser; the study compares 19 variants from eight architecture families across text-only, image-only, and multimodal settings.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Finetuned LLaMA-2-13B achieved the highest OOD overall score at 25.21, narrowly exceeding 2.7B Sheared-LLaMA at 25.02, while Sheared-LLaMA led IID performance at 37.43 versus 37.09. In a 134-turn human-comparison sample, LLaMA-2-13B reached 65.42% of the original navigator’s normalized overall score, whereas GPT-4V reached 31.09%; DMR reduced candidate-ranking time from 916 to 186 ms per turn, approximately a fivefold speedup, while OOD Recall@10 declined from 54.78 to 51.87.
- **Contribution:** The paper formulates real-world conversational web navigation and contributes a large, richly recorded benchmark, action-specific evaluation framework, deliberately structured IID/OOD splits, and efficient methods for representing and retrieving elements from long webpages. Its experiments show that task-specific finetuning and structured HTML inputs matter more than raw model scale or screenshots alone in the evaluated setting.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** WEBLINX uses fixed human trajectories, so it cannot test live interaction, recovery, or alternative execution paths, and single-reference metrics may penalize valid actions or replies. Models receive truncated histories, every finetuned system deteriorates OOD, MiniLM DMR can omit the correct element, each configuration was trained only once, no significance tests are reported, and human agreement was assessed on only 134 validation turns; demonstrations also stop before consequential purchases or bookings.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent safety-evaluation and exposure/background role.** The benchmark is not an attack or evaluated defense, but it documents capabilities relevant to browser-agent risk and warns that unreliable agents can take consequential unintended actions or enable spam, impersonation, and fraud; the released models are not recommended for deployment and should remain under human supervision.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for the formulation and large-scale evaluation of dialogue-conditioned navigation on real websites, especially when discussing expert demonstration data, structured DOM retrieval, turn-level action metrics, OOD generalization, or the gap between finetuned browser agents and human performance.

#### WebVoyager: Building an End-to-End Web Agent with Large Multimodal Models

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../WebVoyager_Building_an_End-to-End_Web_Agent_with_Large_Multimodal_Models.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/webvoyager_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, ACL 2024.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** WebVoyager is a multimodal web-agent capability system and associated live-web benchmark; it belongs in the core/root inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Text-only representations, static snapshots, and simplified simulators omit much of the visual structure and real-world variability involved in browsing. The paper seeks an agent that can complete open-ended tasks end to end on live websites without being constrained to one predefined “golden” action trajectory.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** WebVoyager uses a multimodal model in a Selenium-driven observation–reasoning–action loop, combining screenshots with extracted text and rule-based numbered labels for interactive elements; it supports clicking, input, scrolling, waiting, backtracking, restarting through search, parsing downloaded PDFs, and answering. The authors also construct 643 human-validated tasks across 15 live websites and propose an LMM evaluator that judges outcomes from instructions, answers, and trajectory screenshots.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The principal evaluation covers 643 tasks, with 40–45 tasks per site, under a 1024 × 768 viewport and a 15-step limit; additional tests use 90 GAIA Level 1–2 web tasks and 50 SeeAct online tasks. Human evaluation includes 300 trajectories independently judged by three annotators, while automatic evaluations are repeated three times.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Human evaluation found a 59.1% overall task-success rate for multimodal WebVoyager, versus 40.1% for its text-only variant and 30.8% for GPT-4 with all tools. On the visually complex Google Flights site, success was 59.5%, compared with 7.1% and 2.4% for those baselines, respectively. Full-trajectory GPT-4V judging achieved 85.3% agreement and Cohen’s κ = 0.70 with human judgments, improving from 75.3% agreement and κ = 0.51 when given only the final screenshot.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces an end-to-end agent for live websites that couples screenshot understanding with textual element signals and detector-free numbered-control grounding, alongside a diverse live-web task suite and trajectory-level evaluation protocol. Its experiments provide direct evidence that multimodal interaction substantially improves task completion over accessibility-tree navigation, especially on visually organized interfaces.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The benchmark excludes login- and CAPTCHA-gated sites, covers only 15 selected websites, omits actions such as dragging and media such as video, and caps runs at 15 steps; context clipping, small text, changing webpages, and rule-based grounding also cause repeated navigation and selection errors. Only 22.3% of tasks have stable golden answers, and automatic judging is imperfect and evaluator-dependent: GPT-4V disagreed with humans on 14.7% of full-trajectory cases, while tested evaluators showed differing strictness and some self-preference.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role, not an evaluated defense.** Direct live-web operation exposes agents to malicious downloads, deceptive or manipulated interfaces, unauthorized disclosure through forms, and harmful automated requests or artificial traffic; the study mitigates some risk through non-login tasks, monitoring, and manually screened prompts but does not evaluate prompt-injection resistance or a general security defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when motivating end-to-end multimodal interaction with live websites, numbered visual grounding of browser controls, benchmarks based on complete task success rather than prescribed trajectories, or full-trajectory LMM judging. It is also useful as empirical evidence about failure modes and the expanded security surface of visually grounded web agents.

#### Go-Browse: Training Web Agents with Structured Exploration

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Go-Browse_Training_Web_Agents_with_Structured_Exploration.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/go_browse_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, ICLR 2026 according to the local PDF and OpenReview/arXiv pages.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** Go-Browse is a capability-system training method and data-collection pipeline, accompanied by the Go-Browse-WA dataset and an evaluation of a fine-tuned 7B web agent; it belongs in the core/root inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Independent web-exploration episodes repeatedly revisit easy pages, while tasks proposed from static observations can be narrow, hallucinated, or infeasible. Go-Browse seeks to collect diverse, website-specific interaction data automatically by retaining knowledge of discovered pages and grounding tasks in actual exploration.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** Go-Browse represents each website as a URL graph and combines an outer frontier-search loop with inner page-level task proposal, feasibility checking, and trajectory sampling. It resets agents to discovered pages, uses prefixed sampling to isolate local task execution from difficult navigation, and retains successful demonstrations judged by a vision-language model.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The system explored 20 URLs in each of five self-hosted WebArena domains, producing Go-Browse-WA with 3,422 unique tasks, 26,749 retained trajectories—9,504 successful and 17,245 failed—and 196,462 interaction steps. Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct was fine-tuned on successful trajectories and evaluated on all 812 WebArena tasks, with transfer tested on 300 Online-Mind2Web tasks spanning 136 live sites.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Fine-tuning raised Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct’s WebArena success rate from 8.3% to 21.7%, a statistically significant 13.4-point gain with \(p<0.001\). Go-Browse-7B exceeded NNetNav-7B by 2.9 points on WebArena, although that comparison did not meet the conventional significance threshold (\(p=0.094\)); feasibility filtering removed 403 proposed tasks and avoided about 3,200 rollouts and 29,400 steps, saving 13% of collection steps while preserving the same amount of positive data.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a graph-based exploration and training-data collection system that reuses discovered web states across episodes, grounds task generation in interactive observations, and enables weaker models to contribute demonstrations from deep pages through prefixed sampling. It also releases code, models, and a large dataset containing both successful and failed browser interactions.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Collection covers only five WebArena domains and 100 URLs, relies on proprietary models and an automatically judged reward signal without a reported human-validation rate, and costs approximately $975.57 over about three weeks; each fine-tuning run additionally requires about 40 hours on eight 80-GB H100 GPUs. Go-Browse remains well below larger closed models, underperforms NNetNav on GitLab and genuinely OOD Online-Mind2Web tasks, and its advantages over NNetNav and GPT-4o-mini are not significant at \(p<0.05\); moreover, the conclusion’s “39K unsuccessful trajectories” conflicts with Table 1’s 17,245 failures and 39,339 successful steps.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role, not an attack or evaluated defense.** Its systematic frontier traversal, state reuse, and deeper-page exploration are relevant to crawler authorization boundaries, rate limits, sensitive-state exposure, and adversarial-site risk, but the paper does not evaluate security controls or policy-aware crawling.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper as evidence that structured, reusable website exploration can produce more diverse agent-training data, reduce redundant collection, and substantially improve a small web agent’s in-domain performance. Qualify broader claims because transfer is strongest for sites resembling the training domains and the closest baseline improvements remain statistically uncertain.

#### BEARCUBS: A Benchmark for Computer-Using Web Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../BEARCUBS_A_benchmark_for_computer-using_web_agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/bearcubs_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2025, COLM 2025 according to OpenReview; arXiv preprint also exists.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** BEARCUBS is a benchmark for evaluating computer-using and search-and-reasoning agents on factual information-seeking tasks that require interaction with the live web.
- **Problem and motivation:** Existing web-agent benchmarks often use simulated sites, permit answers to be recovered from HTML or search snippets, or cover only narrow forms of multimodal interaction. BEARCUBS targets realistic, changing websites and deliberately filters out text-only shortcuts so that tasks measure actual browsing and interaction.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The benchmark contains 111 short-answer questions—56 text-based and 55 multimodal—each with a unique gold answer, at least one human-validated trajectory, visited-site records, and a verifiable source or interaction. Questions must be publicly accessible and resistant to prominent search results, snippets, model memory, and, for multimodal tasks, text-only workarounds; the authors intend to replace items as sites change or answers become searchable.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The 111 tasks cover 108 distinct top-level URLs and require a mean of 6.1 steps and 3.4 webpages per viable human trajectory, including scanned documents, dynamic databases, videos, audio, games, maps, and 3D environments. The study compares humans, seven commercial web agents, and five zero-shot or search-augmented baselines, with most computer-use runs limited to 15 minutes and ChatGPT Agent allowed up to 45 minutes.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Humans achieved 84.7% overall accuracy, compared with 65.8% for the strongest agent, ChatGPT Agent, leaving an 18.9-percentage-point gap. The modality gap was larger: humans scored 85.7% on multimodal tasks versus ChatGPT Agent’s 54.5%, while OpenAI Operator scored 12.7%; simple search and memory baselines remained weak, with the best zero-shot baseline, DeepSeek R1, reaching 8.1%.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a continuously refreshed live-web benchmark that couples diverse, difficult-to-bypass interactions with verified answers and human solution trajectories. It also contributes a broad evaluation protocol that examines accuracy, timing, failure behavior, source quality, and trajectory transparency, plus an automatic evaluator reporting 98.2% four-way and 98.7% binary classification accuracy.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The benchmark has only 111 single-short-answer tasks, limited multilingual coverage, no inferential statistical testing, and substantial manual execution and scoring costs; live sites, access restrictions, contamination, and unanticipated textual workarounds can invalidate tasks. Comparisons are also affected by unequal evaluation dates, a 45-minute allowance for ChatGPT Agent versus 15 minutes for most computer-use agents, uneven provider trajectory detail, and internal discrepancies in the reported human split-level errors and Deep Research source percentages.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** BEARCUBS is not an attack or evaluated defense, but its live-web setting exposes agents to untrusted, unstable, or contaminated content and highlights security-relevant concerns including source credibility, access barriers, opaque trajectories, looping behavior, and answers obtained through unintended shortcuts.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite BEARCUBS when motivating or evaluating live-web information-seeking agents, especially claims about the human–agent gap in multimodal computer use, resistance to snippet-based shortcuts, source-grounding quality, or the operational difficulty of maintaining realistic web-agent benchmarks.

#### BrowseComp: A Simple Yet Challenging Benchmark for Browsing Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../BrowseComp_A_Simple_Yet_Challenging_Benchmark_for_Browsing_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/browsecomp_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2025, OpenAI / arXiv-style technical report.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** BrowseComp is a benchmark for persistent, strategic open-web information discovery and belongs in the core/root inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Existing retrieval and question-answering benchmarks often emphasize facts discoverable through brief, straightforward searches and therefore under-measure an agent’s ability to persist, reformulate queries, connect scattered clues, and explore large candidate spaces.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** Human trainers created indirect fact-seeking questions by starting from a known answer and converting several associated properties into entangled constraints. Each item is intended to be hard to discover but easy to grade through a short, stable reference answer, with difficulty screened using contemporary models, basic Google searches, and, for some items, independent human attempts.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The final benchmark contains 1,266 questions across diverse topics; humans attempted 1,255 of them, while five OpenAI model configurations were evaluated using an AI judge for semantic answer equivalence. Additional experiments varied browsing effort, generated up to 64 Deep Research outputs per question, and ran 64 trials per item for Deep Research and o1.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Human trainers solved 367 of 1,255 attempted questions (29.2%) and gave up on 888 (70.8%) after approximately two hours. Deep Research achieved 51.5% accuracy, compared with 9.9% for o1, 1.9% for browsing-enabled GPT-4o, and 0.6% for GPT-4o; selecting the highest-confidence Deep Research answer from 64 samples raised accuracy to approximately 78%. Deep Research nevertheless had a 91% calibration error, the worst reported among the evaluated systems.
- **Contribution:** BrowseComp establishes a 1,266-item, hard-to-find/easy-to-check benchmark for measuring persistent web research, and its experiments isolate the value of strategic reasoning, browsing effort, repeated search trajectories, and answer-selection methods. Its 64-trial difficulty analysis also helped expose and remove 21 defective items from the original 1,287-task collection.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The benchmark deliberately overrepresents obscure factual searches, uses short textual answers rather than realistic ambiguous requests or long-form reports, relies on a model-based grader, and cannot guarantee that every inverted question has only one valid answer. Deep Research was trained on data intended to improve BrowseComp-style performance, its strongest result requires 64 costly attempts, human participants were not professional investigators, and auditing found ambiguity, formatting, and labeling defects even though the identified cases were removed.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** BrowseComp is a capability benchmark rather than an attack, safety evaluation, or evaluated defense; it demonstrates the persistent open-web navigation that can expose agents to malicious or misleading content, but it does not directly measure prompt injection, provenance integrity, browsing-path safety, or resistance to adversarial sources.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite BrowseComp when motivating or evaluating agents that must persistently locate obscure, entangled facts on the open web, when distinguishing mere search access from strategic browsing ability, or when discussing test-time scaling and confidence calibration in deep-research systems.

#### Mind2Web: Towards a Generalist Agent for the Web

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Mind2Web_Towards_a_Generalist_Agent_for_the_Web.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/mind2web_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2023, NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** Mind2Web is a dataset and benchmark for generalist web agents, accompanied by the MindAct capability-system baseline; it belongs in the core/root inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** General-purpose web agents must translate high-level natural-language goals into multistep actions on noisy, dynamic, partially observable websites while generalizing to unfamiliar sites and domains. Earlier benchmarks typically relied on simplified environments, narrow task families, or low-level instructions and therefore did not provide a realistic foundation for this problem.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** Mind2Web contains 2,350 author-verified tasks from 137 real-world websites spanning 31 secondary domains and five top-level categories, with high-level goals, action sequences, and MHTML, DOM/layout, network, and interaction traces. MindAct first uses a fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-base cross-encoder to rank page elements, then asks an LLM to select among shortlisted elements and predict a Click, Type, or Select Option operation through a hierarchical multiple-choice formulation.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The benchmark evaluates independent next-action predictions with correct action histories under Cross-Task, Cross-Website, and Cross-Domain splits; the respective test sets contain 252, 177, and 912 tasks, while training uses 1,009 tasks. Pages average 1,135 elements, tasks average 7.3 actions, and metrics cover element accuracy, operation F1, step success, and strict whole-task success.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** The candidate ranker achieved Recall@50 of 88.9% on Cross-Task, 85.3% on Cross-Website, and 85.7% on Cross-Domain. MindAct with Flan-T5 XL attained the strongest step-success results—52.0%, 38.9%, and 39.6% on those splits, respectively—but whole-task success remained at 5.2%, 5.1%, and 2.9%; the highest task-success value anywhere in the main table was 7.1%, achieved by Flan-T5 Large on Cross-Task.
- **Contribution:** The paper establishes a foundational, publicly released benchmark for learning and testing generalist agents on diverse authentic web interfaces, with explicit generalization tests for unseen tasks, websites, and domains. Its accompanying MindAct experiments provide evidence that small-model element filtering followed by LLM multiple-choice discrimination is substantially more effective than direct element generation.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Mind2Web is primarily English-language and U.S.-oriented, uses web-proficient MTurk annotators, sanitizes pop-ups, CAPTCHAs, advertisements, and other live disruptions, and evaluates cached states that may reject valid alternative action paths. MindAct is text-only, does not directly model page transformations, and is bottlenecked by preprocessing and candidate recall; GPT-4 was evaluated on only 50 tasks per split with ten candidates, while Table 6 also contains an apparent inconsistency between its element-selection caption and values matching step success.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** The work is not a security evaluation or an evaluated defense, but it establishes the capabilities and interaction surfaces of agents that can navigate real websites and potentially perform consequential actions; the paper discusses user confirmation, control, CAPTCHA circumvention, financial transactions, and malicious use as deployment risks.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper as a foundational dataset and benchmark for real-world generalist web-agent training, next-action prediction, and cross-task, cross-website, or cross-domain generalization, or as early evidence for the rank-then-discriminate design used by MindAct.

#### MMInA: Benchmarking Multihop Multimodal Internet Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../MMInA_Benchmarking_Multihop_Multimodal_Internet_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/mmina_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2025, Findings of ACL 2025.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** MMInA is a benchmark and core/root inventory paper for evaluating multimodal Internet-agent capabilities in long, cross-site workflows.
- **Problem and motivation:** Existing web-agent benchmarks often use static or simplified interfaces, short tasks, textual observations, or single-site interactions, whereas realistic online work requires agents to preserve intent, combine visual and textual evidence, and manage ordered subtasks across changing websites.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** MMInA represents browsing as a partially observable process and supplies agents with the task, an accessibility tree, linked viewport images, and action history; agents operate through 12 action types, and ordered endpoint checks score each website-specific hop as `PASS` or `FAIL`. The paper evaluates text, multimodal, and web-oriented agents and additionally tests procedural memory by appending trajectories from the previous \(K\) tasks to the prompt.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The benchmark contains 1,050 human-written tasks across 14 websites, spanning 1–10 hops with a mean of 2.85 hops and 12.9 actions per task; 64.7% of hops require actions and 35.3% are information-seeking. Most sites are evolving real-world services, although the environment also includes an offline standalone shopping site.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Humans achieved 98.43% overall hop success and 96.25% overall task success, compared with GPT-4V’s 21.77% overall task success. GPT-4V’s full-task success declined from 42.91% on single-hop tasks to 3.03% on 2–4-hop tasks and 0% on tasks with at least five hops; even its first-hop success fell from 56.50% in two-hop tasks to 12.50–16.67% in four- to six-hop tasks.
- **Contribution:** MMInA contributes a long-horizon, open-ended benchmark that jointly tests multimodal perception, cross-site navigation, ordered subgoal completion, and workflow memory, while reporting both hop-level progress and complete-task success. Its procedural-memory experiment further shows that replaying a small number of prior trajectories can constrain search, with \(K=2\) typically best, although the plotted gains are not reported as exact values.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Changing websites, protected image access, dated Kiwix URLs, manually defined endpoint keywords, and URL/answer-based checks limit reproducibility and leave individual action quality unevaluated; long-task samples are sparse, no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported, and the memory study evaluates only Gemini-Pro-Vision. The paper also inconsistently reports 2,989 versus 2,991 total hops, and missing input-condition icons in the extracted results prevent reliable mapping of every repeated model row.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** MMInA is a capability benchmark rather than an attack, safety evaluation, or evaluated defense, but its agents consume changing, untrusted webpage text and images across long workflows, making it useful background for studying how hostile content could exploit weak task-flow management and memory.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite MMInA when motivating or evaluating multimodal, multihop Internet agents, especially claims about the gap between isolated web competence and reliable end-to-end execution, the need to distinguish hop success from task success, or the degradation caused by longer workflows.

- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for the BrowserGym–AgentLab research ecosystem, standardized cross-benchmark browser-agent evaluation, reproducibility and trace-analysis infrastructure, or the reported unified comparison of six LLMs/VLMs on web-interaction tasks.

#### WebShop: Towards Scalable Real-World Web Interaction with Grounded Language Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../WebShop_Towards_Scalable_Real-World_Web_Interaction_with_Grounded_Language_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/webshop_comprehensive_summary.md)

- **Problem and motivation:** Existing interactive environments have limited or artificial language, while large NLP benchmarks are generally static and do not ground language in actions, products, images, options, or changing webpages. - **Method / system or benchmark design:** WebShop is an OpenAI Gym-compatible simulated store built from real Amazon products, with search, results, item, and detail pages; agents issue `search[query]` actions and select semantic buttons through `choose[button text]`. Without fine-tuning, IL+RL scored 65.9 with 25% success on Amazon and 62.3 with 21% success on eBay.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a large-scale, automatically scored benchmark that joins query generation, semantic navigation, product comparison, option selection, multimodal grounding, exploration, and memory within one realistic shopping task. It also supplies human demonstrations, diagnostic ablations and oracle analysis, and evidence that policies trained in the simulator can transfer zero-shot to live commercial websites.

- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** WebShop is not a security attack, defense, or safety evaluation; it is an early capability benchmark for reasoning-and-action agents whose semantic web interaction, transfer, and autonomous purchasing behavior provide background for studying later web-agent risks.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when introducing scalable grounded web-interaction benchmarks, shopping-agent evaluation, automatic graded rewards for semantic web tasks, or early evidence of zero-shot transfer from a controlled simulator to Amazon and eBay.

#### AutoScraper: A Progressive Understanding Web Agent for Web Scraper Generation

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../AutoScraper_A_Progressive_Understanding_Web_Agent_for_Web_Scraper_Generation.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/autoscraper_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, EMNLP 2024.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** AutoScraper is a capability system and scraper generator for producing executable, reusable extraction rules; it belongs in the core/root inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Wrapper-based scraping is reusable but requires substantial site-specific engineering and adapts poorly to structural changes, whereas direct LLM extraction handles unfamiliar pages more flexibly but incurs repeated latency and cost. The paper seeks to generate reusable scrapers while addressing long hierarchical HTML, cross-page generalization, and the inadequate visibility that conventional extraction metrics provide into empty or non-running scrapers.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** AutoScraper represents a scraper as an ordered XPath action sequence and progressively alternates top-down selection with step-back traversal to prune the DOM after failed attempts, with at most five retries. It then generates candidates from three seed pages on SWDE and Extended SWDE, tests them across those pages, and uses an LLM discriminator to select the most reusable candidate; the paper also introduces six mutually exclusive executability categories, including Correct and Unexecutable.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The zero-shot evaluation covers eight closed- and open-source LLMs paired with Chain-of-Thought, Reflexion, and AutoScraper across SWDE (320 cases, 32 tasks, and 32,000 evaluated webpages), Extended SWDE (294 cases, 221 tasks, and 29,400 webpages), and DS1 (83 cases, 11 tasks, and a table-reported 186 webpages). Additional studies compare direct LLM extraction, five supervised systems, ablations, efficiency, golden-label generation, action-sequence length, and XPath fragility; DS1 uses one inference page per site and therefore does not evaluate synthesis.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** On SWDE, GPT-4-Turbo with AutoScraper achieved the best overall result of 71.56% fully Correct cases, 4.06% Unexecutable cases, and 88.69 F1. This was 10.13 F1 points above direct GPT-4-Turbo extraction and 2.11 points above the best compared supervised system, WebFormer, although that supervised comparison used an unequal training protocol. Under the reported GPT-4-Turbo timings, reusable scraper generation broke even with repeated direct extraction after an average of 19.5 pages, with subsequent wrapper execution taking 0.21–0.54 seconds per page.
- **Contribution:** The paper formulates LLM-based scraper construction as website-level action-sequence generation and contributes a progressive DOM-navigation algorithm, cross-page candidate synthesis, and an executability evaluation that exposes failures hidden by ordinary precision, recall, and F1. Its experiments distinguish content recognition from structural XPath generation and quantify the accuracy–reuse–cost tradeoff across models and datasets.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** AutoScraper is limited to vertical webpage information extraction and remains vulnerable to incompatible layouts within one site, multi-valued attributes, ambiguous instructions, and XPath predicates tied to seed-page text; seven of eight tested models performed better with direct extraction, and smaller or open-source models retained high unexecutable rates. The study reports no significance tests, confidence intervals, or variance; DS1 synthesis could not be tested, the supervised comparison is not protocol-equivalent, and the source inconsistently reports DS1 as 166 versus 186 pages and describes ten efficiency domains while tabulating eight.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent background capability system.** Automating reusable extraction can increase the scale and efficiency of data collection, making the work relevant to privacy, consent, access control, anti-scraping measures, and abuse governance, but the paper neither presents an attack or safety benchmark nor evaluates a defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper as evidence that progressive DOM reasoning and cross-page synthesis can make powerful LLMs effective at generating reusable scrapers, while emphasizing that the demonstrated capability is narrowly evaluated, highly backbone-dependent, and not accompanied by a substantive treatment of scraping governance or misuse controls.

The paper isolates vertical web traversal as link-based navigation, evidence gathering, and reasoning from a supplied root site.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** WebWalkerQA is a bilingual QA benchmark stratified by page depth, single- versus multi-source evidence, and three difficulty levels. The WebWalker baseline pairs an explorer that selects clickable links with a critic that filters relevant observations into memory, decides when the evidence is sufficient, and generates an answer.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The benchmark contains 680 manually verified English and Chinese QA pairs spanning more than 1,373 webpages in conference, organization, education, and games domains, evenly divided between 340 single-source and 340 multi-source questions. Zero-shot ReAct, Reflexion, and WebWalker agents using six reported closed- and open-source backbones receive the correct root URL and at most 15 actions; accuracy is judged by GPT-4, while action count is averaged only over successful runs.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** GPT-4o WebWalker achieves the highest core-agent accuracy at 37.50%, compared with 33.82% for GPT-4o ReAct, while its largest category gain is on easy multi-source questions, at 47.50% versus 32.50%. Even the best result therefore fails on more than 60% of the benchmark, and the highest hard multi-source accuracy is only 16.67%, achieved by GPT-4o Reflexion.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a benchmark that jointly tests vertical website depth, evidence width across pages, multi-hop link traversal, bilingual QA, and concise answer generation, together with an explorer–critic baseline designed for that setting. It also demonstrates that critic-filtered vertical evidence can complement horizontal RAG retrieval across every tested difficulty category.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** WebWalkerQA has only 680 verified items, uses an HTML-only environment without screenshots, supplies the correct root URL, and evaluates prompted agents under a principal 15-action cap; GPT-4 judging is not independently validated, and no significance tests, confidence intervals, or variance estimates are reported. Reporting inconsistencies include domain percentages totaling more than 100% and a claim of nine validated models despite only six distinct reported backbones; several figure-derived RAG and action-scaling values are only approximate.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Adjacent exposure/background role.** This is primarily a benchmark and capability-system study rather than an attack, safety evaluation, or evaluated defense, but its deeper traversal capability can expose agents to hidden, sensitive, misleading, or malicious content on internal site pages.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when treating vertical, multi-step traversal of a known website as distinct from ordinary search or generic browser-action completion, or when motivating benchmarks and agentic RAG systems that combine horizontal retrieval with deep link-following exploration.

#### ACE: A Security Architecture for LLM-Integrated App Systems

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/ACE_A_Security_Architecture_for_LLM-Integrated_App_Systems.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/ace_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, NDSS 2026.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** ACE is an evaluated defense architecture for securing LLM-integrated application systems against malicious app metadata and outputs; it belongs in the Security inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Conventional LLM-app systems repeatedly expose planning models to untrusted app descriptions and intermediate outputs, allowing malicious apps to manipulate tool selection, disrupt execution, corrupt other apps’ reported results, or exfiltrate sensitive data. Existing isolation and information-flow defenses do not cover all these objectives under a strong attacker who controls both an app’s implementation and metadata.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** ACE separates operation into abstract planning from trusted input, pairwise mapping of abstract capabilities to installed apps, and isolated execution of a fixed, typed plan. It combines a restricted Python subset, static lattice-based information-flow analysis, least-privilege permissions, and orchestrator-managed containers so app output remains typed data rather than instructions that can revise control flow.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The authors tested ACE against three newly demonstrated IsolateGPT attacks, all 1,054 INJECAGENT cases, 2,000 Agent Security Bench trials, and the LangChain Tool Usage benchmark’s Single Tool, Multiple Tool, and Relational Data suites. Evaluated models included GPT-4o, o3-mini, GPT-4.1, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and Qwen-2.5-72B, with additional measurements of API cost and wall-clock runtime.

With GPT-4o or GPT-4.1, it achieved at least 80% overall accuracy on every Tool Usage suite; GPT-4.1 reached 85.7% overall accuracy on Relational Data. ACE also blocked all three newly introduced attacks, and only three of 2,000 ASB trials invoked benchmark-designated attacker apps, selections the authors judged semantically appropriate and unrelated to injected instructions.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces three concrete attacks on IsolateGPT and presents Abstract-Concrete-Execute, an architecture that fixes high-level control flow before exposing execution to untrusted apps. Its distinctive technical contribution is the integration of metadata-independent abstract planning, independent abstract-to-concrete matching, typed isolated execution, and static analysis of direct, implicit, loop-carried, and long-range information flows.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The restricted-Python implementation lacks a formal soundness guarantee, supports only standalone applications and single queries, and retains possible plan-worker escape and incomplete runtime-trace-verification risks. INJECAGENT and ASB use simulated apps and measure expected-app use rather than output correctness; matching can require many LLM comparisons, no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported, detailed ASB results are absent from the supplied text, and Figures 6 and 10 appear to transpose two attack names despite showing the intended containment behaviors.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role: evaluated defense.** ACE is an architectural defense against planning-integrity, execution-integrity, availability, and privacy attacks under weak and strong malicious-app threat models; its isolation, policy enforcement, and information-flow checks are implemented and empirically evaluated rather than merely proposed as mitigations.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite ACE when motivating trusted/untrusted separation, ahead-of-time typed planning, independent tool matching, least-privilege execution, or static information-flow enforcement for web and application agents. Its results support architectural arguments that untrusted website or tool output should not be returned to an LLM with authority to revise system-level control flow, while its single-query and simulated-benchmark constraints should be noted when extrapolating to open-web browser agents.

#### Attention is All You Need to Defend Against Indirect Prompt Injection Attacks in LLMs

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/Attention_is_All_You_Need_to_Defend_Against_Indirect_Prompt_Injection_Attacks_in_LLMs.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/rennervate_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, NDSS 2026.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** RENNERVATE is an evaluated defense against textual indirect prompt injection and belongs in the Security inventory; the paper also introduces the FIPI dataset.
- **Problem and motivation:** External content retrieved by web agents and other LLM applications can contain hidden instructions that redirect the model, leak sensitive information, or hijack the user’s goal. Existing detectors may generalize poorly or reject an entire document, while prevention methods can require target-model modification or leave malicious instructions intact.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** RENNERVATE classifies each external-data token using the target LLM’s internal attention features, aggregates smoothed token predictions into a document-level verdict, and removes predicted injection spans while retaining clean content. Its two-step attentive-statistics pooling weights response tokens and attention heads; the accompanying FIPI dataset contains 100,000 injected and 10,000 benign examples across 300 NLP subtasks, with randomized injection positions and fine-grained labels.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The evaluation covers five structurally different 6B–8B target LLMs, 15 detection or sanitization baselines, FIPI, five unseen task-pair scenarios containing 2,000 examples each, unseen GCG and Neural Exec attacks, and adaptive PAIR, TAP, and white-box GCG adversaries. It measures detection accuracy, false-positive and false-negative rates, attack success, textual preservation, and downstream utility.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** On FIPI, detection accuracy ranged from 97.88% to 99.58% across the five models; on LLaMA3, RENNERVATE achieved 99.37% accuracy, 0.84% FPR, and 0.42% FNR. Sanitization reduced total FIPI attack-success rates from 60.80–85.90% without defense to 0–0.20%. Transfer was less uniform: detection fell to 80.20% for LLaMA3 on MRPC–SST2, and adaptive PAIR retained a 19% attack-success rate on LLaMA3.
- **Contribution:** The paper contributes a compact 0.5–0.8M-parameter defense that uses a target model’s attention behavior to jointly detect, localize, and sanitize indirect prompt injections without retraining that model. It also contributes FIPI and evidence that attention-derived token signals transfer beyond the training distribution to cross-task, gradient-based, and adaptive attacks.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Deployment requires access to internal attention weights and therefore does not directly support closed API-only models; the proposed local shadow-model workaround is not the paper’s primary evaluation setting. Sanitization can leave ordinary-looking injected-task content, harm exact-output tasks, falsely flag legitimate task-like text, or miss short spans because of the consecutive-token threshold; cross-task attack-success judgments also relied on a DeepSeek judge whose manually measured accuracy was 94.50%, and image- and audio-based injections were out of scope.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role.** This is an evaluated textual indirect-prompt-injection defense with a supporting dataset, not merely a detection benchmark or mitigation discussion.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite it when comparing defenses that detect and recover from prompt injection in web agents, especially for attention-based localization, sanitization without target-model retraining, transfer to unseen or adaptive attacks, and the FIPI dataset.

#### AWE: Adaptive Agents for Dynamic Web Penetration Testing

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/AWE_Adaptive_Agents_for_Dynamic_Web_Penetration_Testing.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/awe_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, NDSS LAST-X 2026 / arXiv; main-conference status should not be inferred.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** AWE is an autonomous black-box web penetration-testing capability system focused primarily on injection-related vulnerabilities; it belongs in the Security inventory.
- **Problem and motivation:** Traditional scanners depend on known payloads and signatures, making them brittle against unfamiliar sanitization and application-specific behavior, while unconstrained LLM agents can be expensive, unstable, and difficult to reproduce. AWE addresses the need for a tester that can infer context from noisy HTTP behavior, adapt its payloads, retain state across probes, and verify exploitability rather than merely report suspected weaknesses.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** AWE combines resource-aware orchestration, SQLite-backed short- and long-term memory, reconnaissance, and specialized agents for XSS, SQL injection, SSTI, IDOR, LFI, XXE, SSRF, and command injection. Its agents follow vulnerability-specific probing and mutation procedures, use LLMs within discovered contextual constraints, and require behavioral evidence such as hidden-flag retrieval, differential responses, timing effects, or Playwright-backed browser execution.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The principal evaluation used 104 containerized XBOW applications spanning 26 vulnerability categories, with Claude Sonnet 4, isolated runs, reset memory, a ten-minute limit per challenge, and success defined strictly as retrieving the hidden flag; results were compared with MAPTA’s published per-challenge results. A separate DVWA study tested Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4o, and Gemini 2.0 Flash over five XSS and SQL-injection tasks, with 10 independent trials per model and vulnerability type.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** AWE solved 54/104 XBOW challenges (51.9%), versus MAPTA’s 80/104 (76.9%), but solved successful challenges in 53.1 seconds on average versus 190.8 seconds and was strongest on XSS at 20/23 (87%) versus 13/23 (57%). It used 1.12 million tokens versus MAPTA’s 54.87 million—approximately 98% fewer—and incurred $7.73 in total API cost versus $21.38, approximately 63% less; however, the paper’s stated AWE cost per solve does not reconcile arithmetically with its reported solve count and total cost.
- **Contribution:** The paper demonstrates that a memory-augmented architecture with explicit vulnerability-specific procedures, constrained LLM payload mutation, selective orchestration, and execution-backed verification can outperform a stronger general-purpose agent on targeted classes while sharply reducing runtime, token use, and cost. It also supplies empirical evidence for the complementary trade-off between efficient specialist agents and broader agents capable of open-ended, multi-stage exploitation.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** AWE’s injection-centric scope excludes or poorly covers business logic, cryptographic and protocol-level flaws, complex authentication transitions, privilege escalation, race conditions, and long exploit chains; even its SSTI and command-injection agents trailed MAPTA substantially. Cross-target learning was not tested because memory was reset, MAPTA was not rerun by the authors, hardware details and statistical uncertainty are absent, blind SQL injection had only three XBOW cases, DVWA used only 10 trials per condition, and Table II contains an unexplained cost-per-solve inconsistency.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role: capability system.** AWE performs autonomous web vulnerability discovery and exploitation under a black-box threat model, but it is neither a benchmark nor an evaluated defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when discussing specialist versus general-purpose architectures for autonomous web penetration testing, particularly context-sensitive XSS and blind-SQL-injection workflows, persistent probe memory, browser-backed exploit verification, and the coverage-versus-efficiency trade-off in LLM-guided security testing.

#### Formalizing and Benchmarking Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/Formalizing_and_Benchmarking_Prompt_Injection_Attacks_and_Defenses.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/formalizing_prompt_injection_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, USENIX Security 2024.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is an attack/defense benchmark in the Security inventory, covering prompt injection in general LLM-integrated applications rather than specifically agentic web crawlers.
- **Problem and motivation:** Prompt-injection research lacked both a general formalism relating attacks to one another and a systematic quantitative comparison across tasks, models, and defenses. The paper addresses attacks in which untrusted external data causes an application’s LLM to perform an attacker-selected task instead of its intended task.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The paper formalizes target and injected tasks, represents attacks as constructions of compromised external data, and derives a Combined Attack from context separation, fake completion, and context-ignoring strategies. It benchmarks five attacks and ten defenses—five prevention-based and five detection-based—using metrics for target-task utility, injected-task performance, attack success, response matching, false positives, and false negatives.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The benchmark covers ten LLMs and seven English-language NLP tasks, yielding 49 target/injected-task combinations; each dataset supplies 100 non-overlapping target and injected examples, with 100 target–injected pairs sampled for attack and detection metrics rather than all 10,000 possible pairs. The tasks include classification, grammar correction, and summarization in LLM-integrated applications, not long-horizon browser agents.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Combined Attack achieved a mean Attack Success Value (ASV) of 0.75 on GPT-4 and 0.71 on PaLM 2 across all 49 task pairs; across all ten models, its mean ASV was 0.62 and mean response Matching Rate was 0.78. None of the defenses was uniformly effective: for example, paraphrasing reduced clean-task performance by 0.14 on average, while naive LLM detection reached zero false negatives but produced false-positive rates as high as 0.93 for hate detection and 0.83 for spam detection.
- **Contribution:** The paper provides a formal definition and unifying construction framework for prompt injection, introduces the stronger Combined Attack, and releases a common evaluation platform spanning five attacks, ten models, seven tasks, and ten prevention or detection defenses. It also supplies metrics that distinguish ordinary task ability from attack success and response imitation, plus a clean-data procedure for choosing perplexity-detection thresholds.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The attacks use fixed heuristics rather than optimization, the threat model excludes attackers requiring repeated application queries, and the evaluation is limited to seven English NLP tasks, ten models, and 100 sampled pairs per task combination without significance tests or uncertainty intervals. Perplexity defenses used Llama-2-13B-chat because GPT-4 did not expose perplexity, known-answer detection used one prompt and a seven-character key, and some detailed results and alternative delimiters appear only in a separate technical report unavailable with the supplied paper.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role.** This is an attack benchmark and safety evaluation of proposed defenses; because it quantitatively tests prevention and detection methods, it also provides evaluated-defense evidence, although no tested defense is a general solution.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for a formal threat model of indirect prompt injection, the target-task/injected-task distinction, a systematic cross-model attack benchmark, or evidence about the effectiveness–utility and false-negative–false-positive tradeoffs of prompt-injection defenses.

#### Identifying AI Web Scrapers Using Canary Tokens

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/Identifying_AI_Web_Scrapers_Using_Canary_Tokens.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/canary_tokens_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, arXiv.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is an attribution-measurement capability system in the Security inventory, used to identify the scraper infrastructure supplying web content to production AI chatbots and to evaluate post-scraping access controls.
- **Problem and motivation:** Public crawler disclosures, community lists, and self-reported User-Agent strings are incomplete and vulnerable to spoofing, while third-party search indexes obscure the path from a website to a chatbot answer. The paper seeks a way for ordinary site operators to attribute query-time AI scraping without trusting a crawler’s claimed identity.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The authors serve each distinct User-Agent/ASN pair a unique set of canary-token values embedded across fictitious webpages, query web-enabled chatbots about those entities, and match emitted tokens back to the scraper that received them. - **Evaluation setting and scale:** The study deployed 20 controlled `.com` websites containing 10 token placeholders each and tested 22 production chatbots over several months from a single U.S. university vantage point. Across all sites it recorded 4,042 distinct User-Agent/ASN pairs, averaging 592.2 per site, and queried every chatbot about every site at multiple checkpoints.

Twelve of the 18 attributable systems continued returning content under both offline and restrictive-`robots.txt` conditions, while Duck.ai alone stopped under both controls.

Its controlled deployment reveals declared first-party bots, generic browser-like identities, undocumented third-party search dependencies, and persistence of previously collected content after access is withdrawn.

Persistence cannot distinguish cached content from fresh prohibited crawling, and the source contains an unresolved \(F_\ell(s)\)/\(W_\ell(s)\) notation inconsistency plus a table reporting 129 Grok/Googlebot website visits despite only 20 experimental sites.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role.** This is a scraper-attribution and safety-measurement capability system, not an evaluated defense: it exposes concealed or indirect retrieval paths and tests whether post-scraping controls suppress chatbot outputs, but it does not establish that observed persistence proves new `robots.txt` violations.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite it for canary-token attribution of AI web-retrieval infrastructure, evidence that chatbots may use generic browsers or third-party search crawlers, and experimental findings that taking sites offline or adding `robots.txt` restrictions after collection usually does not remove their content from chatbot answers.

#### Not What You've Signed Up For: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/Not_What_You've_Signed_Up_For_Compromising_Real-World_LLM-Integrated_Applications_with_Indirect_Prompt_Injection.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/indirect_prompt_injection_real_world_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2023, ACM AISec 2023.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is an attack study in the Security inventory that introduces and demonstrates indirect prompt injection against retrieval- and tool-augmented LLM applications.
- **Problem and motivation:** LLM-integrated applications process authorized instructions and untrusted retrieved content in the same textual channel, allowing instructions planted in websites, emails, documents, code, or memory to steer a model remotely. The attacker need not access or control the victim’s LLM directly.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The paper develops a threat taxonomy spanning passive, active, user-driven, and hidden injection methods, then manually constructs proof-of-concept attacks against Bing Chat, GitHub Copilot, and synthetic agents built around OpenAI models. The synthetic agents expose subsets of six prepared interfaces—search, webpage viewing, URL retrieval, email, address-book access, and memory—and are tested at temperature 0.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** This is a qualitative security evaluation rather than a statistical benchmark: attacks were exercised through local HTML in Bing Chat, controlled mock content and tools in synthetic agents, and repository context in GitHub Copilot. The synthetic environment provided six tool interfaces and used sampling temperature 0 for reproducibility; no repeated-trial statistics, user-study measurements, latency results, or numerical baselines were reported.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces indirect prompt injection as a distinct remote attack class, frames retrieved prompts as attacker-supplied programs, and provides a broad taxonomy connecting delivery channels, harms, and affected parties. Its demonstrations establish that injected content can manipulate not only an LLM’s answer but also tool selection, tool arguments, retrieved outputs, persistent memory, and subsequent evidence-seeking.

- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role: attack study.** It is a foundational indirect-prompt-injection paper showing how untrusted retrieved content can remotely compromise LLM-integrated applications; although it discusses possible mitigations, it does not evaluate a defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for the original indirect-prompt-injection threat framing, its threat taxonomy, and early practical evidence that retrieval- and tool-augmented LLM systems can treat attacker-controlled data as executable instructions.

#### Overcoming the Retrieval Barrier: Indirect Prompt Injection in the Wild for LLM Systems

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/Overcoming_the_Retrieval_Barrier_Indirect_Prompt_Injection_in_the_Wild_for_LLM_Systems.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/retrieval_barrier_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, USENIX Security 2026.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is an in-the-wild attack study and direct-security paper in the Security inventory. It evaluates an end-to-end indirect prompt-injection attack against embedding-based retrieval in RAG, single-agent, and multi-agent systems under natural user queries.
- **Problem and motivation:** Prior indirect prompt-injection demonstrations commonly assumed that poisoned content was already in the model’s context, although unoptimized malicious documents were never retrieved in the authors’ initial experiments across 11 benchmarks. The paper asks whether one poisoned item can overcome this retrieval barrier without corpus access, model parameters, or embedding gradients and then cause downstream harm.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The attack separates an arbitrary malicious payload into an attack fragment and prepends a short trigger fragment optimized for query similarity. It adapts the Cross-Entropy Method to search for triggers using only black-box embedding-API responses, with a default 10-token trigger and at most 150,000 embedding evaluations, then tests retrieval, RAG manipulation, agent tool misuse, phishing propagation, and code execution.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The retrieval evaluation injects one malicious document per query for 100 queries from each of 11 BEIR datasets spanning roughly 26,000 to 8.8 million documents, primarily using Recall@5. Additional experiments cover eight embedding models, RAG with 11 datasets and a reported 11 LLMs despite 12 displayed model rows, and Enron-email workflows with ten generated questions, GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini, AutoGen and Magentic-One, four attack objectives, and five random seeds.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** With 10-token CEM triggers, Table 3 reports an average Recall@5 of 94.1%, including 100% on seven datasets and 99.8% on FEVER; the weakest results were 74.0% on MS MARCO and 77.5% on ArguAna. On FiQA, CEM achieved 100% Recall@5 for seven of eight tested embedding models and 90% for OpenAI text-embedding-3-small. In the most severe downstream result, Fusion achieved a GPT-4o multi-agent code-execution ASR of 0.80±0.07, meaning eight of ten runs exfiltrated a private SSH-related file.
- **Contribution:** The paper identifies retrieval as a decisive bottleneck for realistic indirect prompt injection and provides the first reported end-to-end evaluation under natural queries and realistic corpora across RAG, single-agent, and multi-agent systems. Its specific technical contribution is a black-box CEM adaptation that constructs compact, partly transferable retrieval triggers using only embedding-API access, together with evidence that simple retrieval-stage defenses fail under adaptation.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The study covers embedding-based retrieval but not hybrid lexical/vector search or rerankers, and its defense evaluation is limited to the retrieval stage. Agent experiments use one controlled Enron scenario, ten generated questions, two base models, and particular orchestration configurations; cross-architecture transfer is inconsistent, the theoretical guarantee assumes position-wise linear scoring, several proofs and appendices were unavailable, and Table 3’s 94.1% average Recall@5 conflicts with 95.6% stated in nearby prose.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role: attack and safety evaluation.** The work demonstrates that retrieval-optimized indirect prompt injection can manipulate answers, propagate phishing, misuse tools, and induce code execution or SSH-key exfiltration, with multi-agent delegation sometimes amplifying risk by hiding the malicious source from the executing agent; the discussed mitigations are evaluated retrieval-stage defenses, not a successful defense contribution.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for realistic black-box retrieval-optimized indirect prompt injection, the trigger-fragment/attack-fragment decomposition, one-document poisoning across embedding systems, or evidence that multi-agent orchestration can amplify downstream compromise after poisoned content is retrieved.

#### Prompt Injection Attack to Tool Selection in LLM Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/Prompt_Injection_Attack_to_Tool_Selection_in_LLM_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/toolhijacker_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, NDSS 2026.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is a tool-selection attack and attack benchmark in the Security inventory, evaluating whether a malicious third-party tool document can hijack an LLM agent’s retrieval-and-selection pipeline.
- **Problem and motivation:** Agents commonly retrieve candidate tools from potentially untrusted libraries and then let an LLM select one, so a malicious description that is both relevant and persuasive can cause an attacker-controlled tool to be chosen and potentially expose data or enable unauthorized actions.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** ToolHijacker constructs one malicious tool document under a no-box threat model by separating its description into a retrieval component \(R\), optimized to match varied formulations of a target task, and a selection component \(S\), optimized to make the LLM prefer the malicious tool over retrieved competitors. The paper develops gradient-free and gradient-based variants using only a disjoint shadow task set, tool library, retriever, and LLM.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The study covers MetaTool and ToolBench, with 1,000 generated and human-evaluated target descriptions for 10 tasks per dataset, eight target LLMs, four retrievers, and a default top-\(k\) of five. The underlying libraries contain 199 MetaTool tools and 9,650 cleaned ToolBench tools, with additional ablations and evaluations of prevention defenses, automated detectors, and six human reviewers.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** With Llama-3.3-70B as the shadow LLM and GPT-4o as the target, gradient-free ToolHijacker achieved 96.7% attack success rate on MetaTool and 88.2% on ToolBench, versus 39.3% and 58.3% for the strongest reported baseline, PoisonedRAG. A single gradient-free malicious document was retrieved in 99.9% of MetaTool cases and 96.1% of ToolBench cases; on 900 unrelated-task descriptions, its non-target attack success rate was 0%, indicating narrowly targeted behavior.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces ToolHijacker as the first attack designed specifically for the complete tool-selection pipeline, showing that separately optimizing semantic retrieval and LLM selection enables one coherent malicious document to transfer across unknown models, retrievers, task phrasings, retrieval settings, and libraries containing thousands of benign tools. Its extensive ablations and defense studies isolate why both \(R\) and \(S\) are necessary and document the inadequacy of tested prevention, automated-detection, and human-review approaches.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The experiments are controlled and cover tool selection rather than subsequent tool calling or execution; no malicious tools were deployed online, and the threat model assumes that an attacker can publish a tool and construct a shadow pipeline. Results are limited to two benchmarks, 20 designed tasks, eight LLMs, and four retrievers; performance can decline with larger libraries, small retrieval depths, insufficient shadow candidates, weak shadow models, or resistant targets, while the detection study used only 10 malicious documents per dataset and the human study only six participants and seven malicious documents. No confidence intervals, variability estimates, or significance tests are reported, and some experimental materials appear only in a separately referenced technical-report appendix.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role.** This is an attack and attack benchmark for agent tool selection, not an evaluated defense; it demonstrates that retrieval relevance and successful LLM selection do not establish a third-party tool’s trustworthiness or safety.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite it as evidence that semantically aligned prompt injection in tool metadata can reliably redirect agents toward attacker-controlled tools under restrictive no-box conditions, motivating authenticated tool provenance, pre-execution authorization, document validation, and independent verification of selected tools.

#### SafeArena: Evaluating the Safety of Autonomous Web Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Security/SafeArena_Evaluating_the_Safety_of_Autonomous_Web_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/safearena_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2025, ICML 2025 / PMLR.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** SAFEARENA is a safety benchmark for autonomous web agents and belongs in the Security inventory; it also evaluates interactive attacks against agent safety behavior.
- **Problem and motivation:** Safety alignment learned for conversational models may not transfer to autonomous agents that can manipulate realistic websites. The paper asks whether current web agents deliberately comply with harmful instructions while separating genuine restraint from failure caused by weak browser-operating capability.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** SAFEARENA pairs 250 harmful tasks with 250 capability-matched safe tasks across five harm categories and four simulated websites. Claude had the highest normalized safety score at 55.0, whereas Qwen had the lowest at 21.5 and an ARIA refusal rate of only 0.7%. Its attack experiments further demonstrate that apparently strong refusal behavior can collapse under sequential decomposition or when an agent begins partway through a harmful workflow.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** SAFEARENA primarily tests explicit, rudimentary harmful requests in controlled simulated websites where agents possess the required permissions, rather than ambiguous, context-dependent intent or live-service risks. Functional checks can miss off-target harm, GPT-4o supplies much of the automated judgment, human validation covers only two models and the 150 human-written harmful tasks, and no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported; the paper also contains evaluator-dependent completion figures and a Figure 24 caption–metadata category conflict.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role.** This is a web-agent safety benchmark with an embedded attack evaluation: decomposition and priming are evaluated as jailbreak techniques, but no mitigation is established as an evaluated defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite SAFEARENA when motivating or evaluating harmful-task compliance, capability-adjusted safety, refusal behavior, and interactive jailbreak susceptibility in autonomous web agents; specify whether a reported completion rate comes from functional task checks or ARIA judgment.

Its evaluation shows that goal-directed crawling exposes a distinct, deeper attack surface and can complement the broader but shallower coverage of conventional crawlers.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Task generation hallucinated nonexistent functionality in 543 cases, execution frequently failed through missing prerequisites, premature stopping, or deviation, and simple CRUD ordering could permit destructive state changes such as deleting the only account. The evaluation used only 20 locally hosted applications, manually supplied authentication, URL/form counts rather than semantic state coverage, GPT-4 selected through a small comparison without per-model scores, and only Black Widow’s XSS engine; WordPress exceeded the prompt limit, one reported Moodle issue was attribute injection rather than direct XSS, stochastic reruns may differ, and vetted-only code access restricts reproducibility.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct security role; capability system.** YURASCANNER is an evaluated web application scanner that uses LLM-guided workflow traversal to reach and dynamically test security-relevant forms; it is not itself a benchmark or an evaluated defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when motivating task-driven web security crawling, describing an LLM agent that automatically generates and executes application workflows, or supporting the claim that goal-directed and conventional crawling uncover complementary attack surfaces.

#### AndroidWorld: A Dynamic Benchmarking Environment for Autonomous Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/AndroidWorld_A_Dynamic_Benchmarking_Environment_for_Autonomous_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/androidworld_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2025, ICLR 2025.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** AndroidWorld is an adjacent GUI-agent benchmark in the Borderline inventory, relevant for comparing web-agent evaluation with realistic mobile-device control.
- **Problem and motivation:** Action-sequence matching cannot reliably establish whether an autonomous agent completed a task because valid paths may differ, applications may behave non-deterministically, and agents may recover from mistakes. AndroidWorld instead supports reproducible, outcome-based evaluation in functioning Android applications.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The environment provides 116 parameterized task templates across 20 real Android apps, with seeded setup and teardown, fixed app and OS configurations, screenshots and accessibility-tree observations, and touch-oriented actions. It calculates automatic rewards by inspecting application databases, files, system settings, and, when necessary, visible UI state; the paper also introduces the zero-shot M3A agent and a 92-task Android adaptation of MiniWoB++.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The principal evaluation ran human, M3A, M3A-Simple, and Android-adapted SeeAct baselines on all 116 AndroidWorld tasks and a standard 62-task subset of MobileMiniWoB++, using one primary seed and task-specific step limits generally set near twice human requirements. Robustness experiments additionally evaluated three seeds, 20 trials under fixed and varied parameters for each of five selected tasks, and a second device configuration.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Text-only M3A with GPT-4 Turbo achieved the best AndroidWorld agent success rate, **30.6%**, versus **80.0%** for humans, a **49.4-percentage-point** gap. On AndroidWorld, full text-only M3A outperformed M3A-Simple by **10.8 points** (30.6% versus 19.8%), while its results across three seeds ranged from **26.3% to 33.2%**, with a **29.0% mean**, demonstrating sensitivity to task parameterization.
- **Contribution:** The paper contributes a reproducible, dynamically parameterized Android benchmark whose rewards measure actual device outcomes rather than imitation of recorded interactions, together with reusable validators, baseline agents, MobileMiniWoB++, and empirical evidence that single-instance scores can obscure substantial variation in agent capability.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Coverage is restricted mainly to fixed-version open-source and built-in apps that require no login and store data locally, limiting generalization to proprietary commercial applications. The headline score uses one seed; the controlled robustness analysis covers only five tasks, MobileMiniWoB++ results cover 62 of 92 supported tasks, accessibility trees can be incomplete, and cross-device testing includes only one additional device/OS configuration.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Borderline/adjacent benchmark role.** AndroidWorld is not an attack or evaluated defense, but its realistic state-changing actions, cross-app workflows, and documented risks such as spam, CAPTCHA bypass, and malicious prompt or screen manipulation make it useful background for reasoning about the capabilities and hazards of web and GUI agents.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite AndroidWorld when motivating outcome-based, dynamically parameterized evaluation of computer-control agents, comparing browser agents with broader GUI agents, or arguing that robustness should be measured across multiple task instances and configurations.

#### BackdoorAgent: A Unified Framework for Backdoor Attacks on LLM-based Agents

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/BackdoorAgent_A_Unified_Framework_for_Backdoor_Attacks_on_LLM-based_Agents.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/backdooragent_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, Findings of ACL 2026.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** Borderline cross-stage agent-backdoor attack benchmark; it directly evaluates a multimodal Agent Web setting but more broadly covers planning, memory, and tool or environmental attacks across several agent domains.
- **Problem and motivation:** Component-local backdoor studies do not capture how malicious triggers can persist in plans, retrieved memories, tool outputs, or observations and then propagate through recurrent context and state updates. The paper therefore treats agent compromise as a trajectory-level integrity problem rather than an isolated malicious response.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** BackdoorAgent provides stage-aware hooks for planning, memory retrieval, and tool execution without imposing a rigid control flow, records complete structured trajectories, and standardizes prompting, retrieval, tool-call handling, replay, and evaluation. It maps seven representative attacks into a shared taxonomy and measures clean accuracy, attack success rate, and accuracy under attack.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The benchmark covers Agent QA, Agent Code, Agent Web, and Agent Drive under language-only and multimodal conditions, using GPT, Claude, Gemini, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Kimi model families; Agent Web is limited to multimodal-capable backbones. The paper does not report sample counts, uncertainty intervals, repeated-run variability, or significance tests.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** For the GPT family, average attack success rate was 43.58% through planning, 77.97% through memory, and 60.28% through tools, making memory the most vulnerable injection channel. In Agent Web, Gemini 3 Flash reached 95.16% ASR for PoisonedRAG and 95.40% for DemonAgent while attacked task accuracy remained at least 92.41%, whereas GPT-4o-mini’s listed Web ASRs stayed between 0% and 8.91% despite similarly high clean accuracy. Increasing GPT memory-corpus poisoning from 0.1 to 0.5 raised ASR monotonically from 53.06% to 97.72%.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a modular, stage-aware framework and standardized attack benchmark for tracing where agent backdoors enter, activate, persist, and propagate across planning, memory, tools, and environmental feedback. Its cross-domain evidence distinguishes ordinary task success from behavioral integrity and shows that injection channel and workflow dynamics can predict vulnerability better than model capability alone.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The controlled benchmark does not cover the full diversity of deployed architectures, environments, threat models, adaptive adversaries, or complex cross-stage interactions, and Agent Web has narrower backbone coverage than the other tasks. Results vary sharply by model and task, while absent sample sizes and statistical uncertainty limit generalization; several plotted overhead and probability values also lack exact labels.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Borderline direct attack and attack-benchmark role.** It is directly relevant to browser-agent security through Agent Web and more generally demonstrates persistent memory poisoning, deceptive tool or observation feedback, and cross-stage trigger propagation; its probability-based detector analysis is preliminary and does not constitute an evaluated defense contribution.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for a unified taxonomy and benchmark of workflow-level agent backdoors, evidence that malicious behavior can coexist with high task accuracy, and the need to assess complete trajectories and state evolution rather than treating clean utility as proof of agent integrity.

#### EvoCrawl: Exploring Web Application Code and State using Evolutionary Search

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/EvoCrawl_Exploring_Web_Application_Code_and_State_using_Evolutionary_Search.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/evocrawl_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2025, NDSS 2025 according to targeted official verification.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** EvoCrawl is a stateful black-box security crawler and capability system in the Borderline inventory. It provides non-LLM prior art for automated exploration of web application code and server-side states.
- **Problem and motivation:** Vulnerable code may become reachable only after a scanner performs correctly ordered browser interactions, supplies suitably formatted inputs, and creates prerequisite server-side objects. Existing crawlers can miss these states by filling every field, ignoring element dependencies, or exhaustively enumerating combinations of forms and JavaScript events.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** EvoCrawl combines a breadth-oriented page-collection module with evolutionary search over fine-grained interaction sequences, using browser and database feedback to reward form submissions, filled inputs, uploads, and triggered events. It dynamically infers element dependencies, permits optional fields to be omitted, and integrates modular XSS and three-user sitemap-replay IDOR detectors.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The authors compared EvoCrawl with BlackWidow, JAK, and CrawlJAX on 10 web applications, allocating two processes per crawler and 24 hours per experiment; EvoCrawl was run five times because its search is randomized. Coverage used executed source-code lines, while separate experiments measured successful HTML-form submissions and detection of known and previously unknown vulnerabilities.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** EvoCrawl increased code coverage by 59% on average and achieved 6%–192% greater coverage than BlackWidow across individual applications. It submitted 210 forms uniquely and 55 also submitted by BlackWidow, versus BlackWidow’s 57 unique and 55 shared submissions; the paper separately characterizes POST submission frequency as five times that of the next-best tool. EvoCrawl also found eight zero-day vulnerabilities—five XSS and three Type 2 IDOR flaws—of which six were acknowledged and confirmed by developers.
- **Contribution:** The paper reframes web crawling as evolutionary search for many useful, state-changing interaction sequences rather than simple link traversal or payload evolution. Its specific advances are dependency-aware mutation, field-subset exploration, coordinated breadth/depth crawling, and an IDOR detector that combines lockstep multi-user replay with three-user response comparison.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** EvoCrawl cannot handle unrecognized mandatory input formats, specialized XSS sanitizer bypasses, or workflows requiring logout and re-login; its fixed fitness weights may require application-specific tuning, token handling assumes persistent URL parameters, and setup requires automatic-login configuration plus three registered users. The evaluation covered 10 applications under a 24-hour limit, excluded sensitive pages, omitted JAK and CrawlJAX results for two token-incompatible applications, did not name the statistical test behind reported p-values, and contains source inconsistencies in benchmark versions, one phpBB URL total, the status of a GitLab fix, and the relationship between detailed form counts and the five-times headline claim.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Borderline/adjacent capability-system role.** EvoCrawl directly supports vulnerability discovery, but it is not an LLM or agentic-AI system; here it serves as a strong non-LLM baseline for stateful exploration, XSS reachability, and access-control testing rather than as an evaluated defense.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite EvoCrawl when motivating dependency-aware, state-changing web exploration or when comparing LLM-guided security agents with evolutionary black-box crawling. Its results are especially useful evidence that reaching vulnerabilities often depends more on discovering valid interaction sequences and application states than on increasing payload sophistication.

#### Gorilla: Large Language Model Connected with Massive APIs

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/Gorilla_Large_Language_Model_Connected_with_Massive_APIs.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/gorilla_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2024, NeurIPS 2024.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is an adjacent tool-use capability system and benchmark in the Borderline inventory. It studies single-call API selection and invocation rather than web navigation or crawling.
- **Problem and motivation:** LLMs must select suitable tools, generate exact function names and arguments, adapt to changing documentation, and avoid inventing APIs across large, overlapping tool collections. Retrieval alone is insufficient because an irrelevant top-ranked document can mislead a model, while real requests may also impose accuracy, size, latency, memory, or cost constraints.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** Gorilla is an instruction-fine-tuned LLaMA-7B API-calling model, with a Retriever-Aware Training variant taught to use relevant retrieved documentation and resist irrelevant documentation. The paper also introduces APIBench, comprising a reported 1,645 machine-learning APIs and 16,450 synthetic instruction–API pairs from Torch Hub, TensorFlow Hub, and HuggingFace, plus an AST-subtree evaluator that distinguishes correct calls, incorrect uses of real APIs, and hallucinated APIs.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** Gorilla was evaluated against LLaMA-7B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude, and DocPrompting on held-out APIBench examples under zero-shot, BM25, embedding-based GPT-Index, and oracle-document conditions. Additional experiments examined numerical-constraint satisfaction, test-time documentation changes, three-shot prompting, transfer across base models, and manual execution of 100 randomly selected Gorilla generations.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Without retrieval, ordinary Gorilla attained 59.13%, 71.68%, and 83.79% API accuracy on Torch Hub, HuggingFace, and TensorFlow Hub, with hallucination rates of 6.98%, 10.95%, and 5.40%, respectively. With GPT-Index retrieval, RAT-trained Gorilla reached 61.82%, 47.46%, and 64.96% accuracy, while oracle documentation raised those results to 67.20%, 91.26%, and 94.16%, demonstrating that retrieval quality was a major bottleneck. On constraint-aware Torch Hub examples, Gorilla achieved 47.88% zero-shot constraint accuracy, compared with 71.83% general API accuracy on the same condition. In the 100-generation validation sample, AST accuracy and human API-selection accuracy both equaled 78%, but only 72% of complete generated programs executed successfully.
- **Contribution:** The paper contributes Gorilla as a specialized API-calling model, Retriever-Aware Training for incorporating changing documentation at inference time, and APIBench with an AST-based evaluation framework that separates invented APIs from wrong selections among real APIs. It further provides evidence about constraint-aware selection, retrieval failure modes, test-time adaptation to API and repository changes, and the gap between correct tool selection and executable supporting code.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** APIBench covers only machine-learning APIs from three hubs, evaluates single calls rather than multi-step workflows, retrieves only one document, and includes inconsistent source counts—94 versus 95 Torch Hub APIs and 626 versus 696 Tensor Hub APIs. HuggingFace baseline evaluation is not fully comparable because non-Gorilla systems were assessed mainly on domain selection; the RAT-trained model scored approximately zero without retrieval in the expected format; AST validation covered only 100 Gorilla outputs; and the experiments report no error bars or significance tests. Structurally correct calls can still occur within broken programs, and the demonstrated adaptation to changing documentation is based on selected examples rather than a large temporal-robustness benchmark.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Borderline/adjacent role.** This is a capability system and benchmark, not an attack, safety evaluation, or evaluated defense; its relevance is that more capable API invocation expands an agent’s ability to act through external services and therefore heightens permission, authorization, hallucination, and unsafe-tool-selection risks, while the paper does not evaluate concrete safeguards.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for API/tool-use background, retrieval-aware API invocation, API hallucination measurement, and benchmark design for agents that must choose among changing tools. Do not cite it as evidence about web-navigation performance, multi-step web workflows, or an evaluated security defense.

#### SkillTrojan: Backdoor Attacks on Skill-Based Agent Systems

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/SkillTrojan_Backdoor_Attacks_on_Skill-Based_Agent_Systems.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/skilltrojan_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, ICML 2026 / PMLR 306 according to the local proceedings PDF.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is a direct execution-layer and skill-supply-chain attack paper, placed in Borderline because its experiments center on code, SQL, and software-engineering agents rather than web crawling specifically.
- **Problem and motivation:** Reusable third-party skills are persistent executable packages that agents commonly trust and audit mainly for advertised functionality, creating a supply-chain surface outside model weights, training data, prompts, and transient tool descriptions. A compromised skill can preserve correct visible behavior while causing hidden side effects across later runs and deployments.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** SkillTrojan modifies useful skills so that a trigger causes ordinary tool calls to emit indexed fragments of an encrypted payload into run-local trajectory fields; a verifier collects the fragments in any order, decrypts and executes the payload, and removes intermediate artifacts without disrupting response generation. The accompanying SkillTrojanX dataset contains more than 3,000 backdoored variants synthesized from 1,200 popular marketplace templates across six categories, four payload families, three cryptographic variants, three fragment-count variants, and 50 triggers.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The primary evaluation uses an EHR SQL task with four open-weight and five closed-weight models, a default 20% poisoning ratio, three fragments, and externally verified execution-side effects; clean SQL correctness is assessed with an LLM judge. Additional experiments cover SWE-Bench Verified and an industry-oriented coding benchmark spanning Claude-Code, IFlow, OpenHands, and OpenClaw, while scanner tests use approximately 500 sampled skills and two lightweight guard models.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** On EHR SQL with GPT-5.2, SkillTrojan reached 97.2% attack success rate while retaining 89.3% clean accuracy, versus 58.3% ASR for the strongest competing attack; on SWE-Bench Verified with the same model, it achieved 92.8% ASR and 70.4% accuracy, compared with 70.6% non-attack accuracy. Across the four-framework coding experiment, ASR ranged from 63.53% to 100%, and the two lightweight scanners classified 98.2% and 99.4% of complete SkillTrojan packages as safe.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a systematic backdoor attack on reusable skill implementations that combines dormant trigger activation, encrypted fragmentation, order-independent reconstruction, hidden side-effect execution, and cleanup while leaving the model and ordinary prompts unchanged. It also contributes SkillTrojanX and cross-model, cross-task, and cross-framework evidence that individually plausible skills can cooperate to conceal executable malicious behavior.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The most detailed benchmark is EHR SQL, where correctness relies on an LLM judge, and the paper reports no significance tests, confidence intervals, or repeated-run variance; the broader coding and framework evaluations receive less methodological detail. Activation requires installation, a triggered trace that reaches every fragment producer and verifier, and sufficient execution permissions; the threat model excludes model, operating-system, remote-service, and cross-query-storage compromise, while added tool calls may expose the attack and the experiments do not establish bypass of every sandbox, permission policy, or scanner.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Direct attack role.** The paper demonstrates a persistent skill-supply-chain backdoor whose malicious effect occurs in the execution layer despite a correct final answer, making it especially relevant to web agents that install, compose, or execute third-party skills.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper when motivating signed package provenance, marketplace review, least-privilege execution, sandboxing, cross-skill static and dynamic analysis, trajectory monitoring, and verification of external side effects. It is evidence for treating installed skill code and composed execution behavior—not merely prompts and final responses—as security-critical surfaces.

#### Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/Toolformer_Language_Models_Can_Teach_Themselves_to_Use_Tools.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/toolformer_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2023, NeurIPS 2023.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is an adjacent tool-use capability system in the Borderline inventory. It provides methodological background for autonomous API selection and invocation but does not study web crawling, browser interaction, or web security.
- **Problem and motivation:** Large language models lack current information and remain unreliable at factual recall, exact arithmetic, translation, and temporal reasoning, while prior tool-use approaches typically require substantial supervision or task-specific demonstrations. The paper asks whether a model can instead teach itself when to call external tools, which tool to select, what arguments to supply, and how to use the returned result.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** Toolformer uses a pretrained language model to generate candidate textual API calls from a few demonstrations, executes them, and retains calls whose results reduce loss on subsequent tokens; it then fine-tunes the original model on the augmented corpus. The principal 6.7B-parameter GPT-J system learns to use question answering, Wikipedia search, calculator, translation, and calendar APIs without task-specific tool-use demonstrations at evaluation time.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The paper conducts prompted zero-shot evaluations on factual completion, mathematical reasoning, open-domain and multilingual question answering, temporal reasoning, and language-modeling perplexity, with at most one API call per input. It compares API-enabled and disabled Toolformer variants with GPT-J baselines, 66B-parameter OPT, and 175B-parameter GPT-3, studies scaling from 124M to 6.7B parameters, and introduces the 9,400-question DATESET temporal benchmark.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** On LAMA’s SQuAD, Google-RE, and T-REx subsets, Toolformer scores 33.8, 11.5, and 53.5, improving by 11.7, 5.2, and 18.6 points over the API-disabled Toolformer baseline. On ASDiv, SVAMP, and MAWPS mathematics tasks, API access raises scores from 14.8, 6.3, and 15.0 to 40.4, 29.4, and 44.0; on DATESET, it raises accuracy from 5.9 to 27.3, with the calendar used on 54.8% of examples. Tool-use benefits begin to emerge at approximately 775M parameters in the tested model family.
- **Contribution:** The paper introduces a self-supervised method for learning multi-tool API use by filtering model-generated calls according to their effect on future-token prediction loss. It shows that one 6.7B model can autonomously select among five tools, achieve large zero-shot gains without additional task-specific demonstrations, preserve disabled-tool perplexity relative to ordinary CCNet fine-tuning, and sometimes outperform models ten to twenty-five times larger.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** Toolformer cannot chain calls, inspect multiple search results, reformulate queries, or conduct interactive tool use, and the evaluation’s one-call limit further excludes multi-step strategies. Results are sensitive to prompts, scale, imperfect loss-based filtering, simple BM25 retrieval, tool-specific evaluation restrictions, and CCNet distribution shift; calendar training relies on URL-derived dates available for only about 18% of documents, and language-model preservation is measured only with APIs disabled.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Borderline/adjacent capability-system role.** The work does not present an attack, security benchmark, safety evaluation, or evaluated defense, but its mechanism for autonomous external-tool selection is relevant background for understanding how agentic systems may acquire externally consequential capabilities.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper as foundational evidence that language models can learn autonomous textual API use through self-generated, loss-filtered training examples, especially when discussing self-supervised tool selection, scale-dependent emergence of tool use, or the limits of single-call systems.

#### Toward Secure LLM Agents: Threat Surfaces, Attacks, Defenses, and Evaluation

- **Local sources:** [PDF](<../Borderline/Toward_Secure_LLM_Agents_Threat_Surfaces_Attacks_Defenses_and_Evaluation.pdf>) · [comprehensive summary](../Summaries/toward_secure_llm_agents_comprehensive_summary.md)
- **Year / venue:** 2026, arXiv:2606.10749; the local manuscript says submitted to ACM TOSEM, not a confirmed publication. The comprehensive summary instead identifies it as an ACM TOSEM publication from January 2026, so the publication status should be verified before citation.
- **Scope / role in this repository:** This is a Borderline agent-security survey that supplies lifecycle and systems vocabulary for modeling browser-agent threats. It is a structured literature synthesis, not an attack, benchmark, capability system, or evaluated defense.
- **Problem and motivation:** LLM agents mix untrusted natural-language data with executable control while exercising delegated authority through tools, memory, and coordination. The paper addresses a fragmented literature in which prompt injection is prominent but persistent-state corruption, privilege misuse, multi-agent propagation, defense tradeoffs, and evaluation gaps are less systematically connected.
- **Method / system or benchmark design:** The authors organize agent security around the lifecycle model \(A=\langle I,P,D,T,M,O,C\rangle\), covering input, planning, decisions, tools, memory, outputs, and coordination through the lenses of information flow, delegated authority, and persistent state. They construct the corpus using six literature sources, bounded GPT-5.4-assisted candidate expansion, citation snowballing, manual screening, bibliographic normalization, and multi-label coding.
- **Evaluation setting and scale:** The review covers work dated January 1, 2023 through April 27, 2026: 275 records entered detailed auditing, duplicate versions were merged, and 247 papers formed the reported corpus. It provides descriptive corpus and benchmark analyses rather than a new empirical web-agent benchmark or controlled security experiment.
- **Main exact quantitative findings:** Prompt injection was coded as a threat model in 142 papers, including 86 addressing indirect prompt injection, while tool-use security appeared in 156 papers. Single-agent systems accounted for 200 of 247 papers (80.97%), leaving multi-agent settings at 47 papers (19.03%); 169 papers (68.42%) were arXiv preprints. AgentDojo was the most reused benchmark but represented only 30 occurrences, or 12.82% of benchmark annotations, indicating substantial evaluation fragmentation.
- **Contribution:** The paper contributes an auditable 247-paper corpus and a lifecycle-centered synthesis that unifies prompt injection, privileged tool execution, delayed memory poisoning, and inter-agent spread as failures across information, authority, and state transitions. It further maps defense families to intervention points and develops a software-engineering agenda around provenance, least privilege, containment, regression assurance, and deployment-specific assurance cases.
- **Limitations and validity caveats:** The selection flow contains an unexplained arithmetic inconsistency: 275 audited records minus 25 exclusions equals 250, although the paper reports 251 provisionally retained before merging four duplicates into a 247-paper corpus. Coding involved judgment without preserved parallel annotations or an inter-rater agreement statistic; exact retrieval replay is unavailable, 68.42% of the corpus consists of mutable preprints, and reported frequencies measure research attention rather than effectiveness, prevalence, consequence, or deployment readiness.
- **Security relevance and role:** **Borderline survey role:** it provides a systems-oriented threat model and evidence map for browser-agent security, emphasizing control-flow integrity, capability boundaries, state integrity, and propagation. Its proposed layered defenses and scenario-specific stacks are literature-derived interpretations, not experimentally validated defenses or established standards.
- **How to use / cite:** Cite this paper for lifecycle-based agent threat modeling, corpus-level evidence about research concentration, and terminology connecting indirect injection, tool misuse, memory poisoning, and multi-agent propagation. Cite the underlying empirical papers or benchmarks for claims about a particular attack’s success or a defense’s effectiveness, and verify the TOSEM publication status before finalizing bibliographic metadata.
